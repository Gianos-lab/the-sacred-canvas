So here we are!

In the [first episode](/blog/e1-why-im-building-this-world) of this worldbuilding adventure I explained why I'm building a World. I made it sound exciting: cosmology that shapes language, sounds that determine which gods hear you. Grand ambitions. Standing ovation. Credits roll.

Except the credits didn't roll. Now I'm actually here. Staring at a blank page. And the honest question is: where do I even start?

This is the part no one talks about. The awkward silence after the pitch. The moment when the client says "great, we'll look forward to the deliverable" and you realize you have to actually make the thing.

Okay. Deep breath. Here's what I did.

# Week One: Divergence

I approached this the way I approach new problems at work: structured chaos.

There's a method called **brainstorming** - yes, that overused corporate word - but [IDEO U](https://www.ideou.com/blogs/inspiration/7-simple-rules-of-brainstorming) turned it into something precise. Seven rules. The ones that matter here: defer judgment, encourage wild ideas, go for quantity. No filtering. No "that won't work." Just collect. Evaluation comes later.

I gave myself one week. No constraints. No structure. No attempt at coherence or intrinsic logic. Just raw input.

worlds I love filled a folder: Tatooine's street markets and cantina deals. Jedha's temple desert. Coruscant stacked on itself, the lower you go the darker it gets. Middle-earth's deep time: ages of history visible in ruins and names. Menzoberranzan's inverted city, politics as survival. Arrakis and its economy of scarcity. Hogwarts sitting hidden in a train station.

Different worlds. Different hooks. But patterns started to show: I'm drawn to layers. Hidden depths. Surfaces that aren't the whole story. I sketched early conlang ideas. Phonology experiments. Rough thoughts about how sound, meaning, and power might relate. I mapped consonants to cosmological forces. It felt productive.

Then the week ended. I had what I needed: raw material. Unfiltered. Unjudged.

All of this gets used later. The worlds become reference points. The phonology sketches become a language. The half-formed instincts become design decisions. But not yet.

First, I needed to figure out where to point all of it.

# Week Two: Convergence

> **Before you can build a World, you have to know where the world begins.**

Brainstorming only works if it's followed by structure. Otherwise you loop forever. So I stopped generating and started organizing. This is the next phase: take the chaos and find the shape inside it.

But here's the problem.

I had plenty of material. No way to choose one direction over another. Every idea spawned ten more. Gods led to cosmology. Cosmology led to language. Language led back to gods.

I thought for a while what I was missing. Then, right as I finished my morning coffee it hit me: **the absence of constraints, aka rules, aka laws**.

# The Missing Piece

Then I recognized a pattern from work.

The hardest problems don't get solved by jumping to solutions. First you understand the project goal. Then you lock the **assumptions** and **constraints**. Only then you start designing, otherwise you're improvising, delivering overdue, and explaining to clients why the central bank is fining them.

I had a similar situation here. I couldn't lock anything because everything I'd been brainstorming was a consequence of something else. Gods, magic, language, history: all downstream. Without rules I couldn't find a coherent connection among any of it.

Brainstorming gives you ideas. But to actually *design*, you need assumptions and constraints. Just like at work.

So I went looking for a rule.

# Finding the Rule

But which rule?

I couldn't just pick one. If I grabbed a rule that was *itself* a consequence of something deeper, I'd build the whole World on it, and find out later I'd trapped it in an inconsistency.

I needed a rule that had nothing above it. Something foundational enough that everything else could flow from it without contradiction. But you can't just pick a rule out of thin air. How would I know if it's truly foundational and not just another consequence in disguise?

The only way I could think to get there: find the **most fundamental question** I could ask about existence. Whatever answers that question, that becomes *The Rule* of the World I'll build.

And since there's a discipline that's been asking existential questions for something like 2,500 years, I figured someone might know better than me.

# Finding the Question

I started researching. If philosophers had been asking existential questions for millennia, surely someone had figured out which one comes first. They actually had.

In 1697, [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nothingness/) - German philosopher, mathematician, and the guy who invented calculus alongside Newton - wrote: "The first question that should rightly be asked is":

> **Why is there something rather than nothing?**

Two centuries later, Martin Heidegger - one of the most influential philosophers of the 20th century - called it "the fundamental question of metaphysics." This question keeps appearing because it assumes almost nothing. Just one brute fact: *there is something*.

Every other question - what is reality made of, who created it, what laws govern it - already takes existence for granted.

# The Problem with "Why"

This looked right. But something bothered me.

The word "why" isn't neutral. "Why" asks for a reason. A reason is always a reason *for* something. Which means "something" is already baked into the question.

If you're asking about the origin of existence, you can't have existence already built into the question. Reading more, I discovered Leibniz di did it on purpose as he was looking for God, and "why" is the kind of question that leads there.

So I reframed:

> **How is there something rather than nothing?**

Same question. No hidden assumption.

And here we have it. The question to start building the World.

# What the Question Actually Did

During my week of brainstorming, I'd already sketched an answer: an entity, a primordial state, the cause of everything. It felt intuitive. First thing, everything flows from it. Done.

But the question "how is there something rather than nothing" introduced a concept I hadn't considered: **nothingness as a prior state.** Was there nothing before this entity? Did it emerge from nothing? Or was it itself the nothing: something unstable that couldn't hold?

I don't know yet.

Before I answer, I want to look at how others have answered. Every culture has a creation story. Every cosmology deals with this transition somehow.
